Reset Expectations

What buyers think vs. what's actually true.

Most of the lock-vs-float advice you've heard is wrong. Let's clear that out before we build the framework.

What buyers think

✕

"The Fed cut rates — my mortgage rate will drop tomorrow."

✕

"My rate is locked the moment I'm pre-approved."

✕

"If rates drop after I lock, I'm stuck."

✕

"Floating is always cheaper than locking."

✕

"Good economic news is good for my rate."

What's actually true

✓

The Fed Funds Rate doesn't directly set mortgage rates. The bond market does.

✓

The lock is a separate, explicit step — usually after contract acceptance.

✓

Many lenders offer a one-time float-down if rates drop after lock.

✓

Floating means taking market risk — sometimes profitable, sometimes painful.

✓

Strong economy = higher mortgage rates (Fed will fight inflation).

1The Basics

Lock or float — what each one actually is.

Before any framework, the definitions have to be crisp. These are the two doors. You pick one.

to lock
verb · finance

To secure today's interest rate for a fixed window (usually 30, 45, or 60 days). If market rates rise, you're protected. If they fall, you generally keep the higher rate — unless your lender offers a float-down.

Feels like: Buying insurance. You pay a small premium (built into the rate) for certainty. Most buyers should lock.

to float
verb · finance

To not lock — leaving your rate exposed to daily market moves until you choose to lock or until your lender requires you to. Your rate is whatever the market gives you on lock day.

Feels like: Holding a stock you have to sell soon. Upside is real, downside is real, and you can't control the timing perfectly.

When the lock decision actually happens
  • 1

    Application (Day 0)

    Pre-approval pulls credit and verifies income, but doesn't lock anything. The rate on your pre-approval is an estimate, not a guarantee.

  • 2

    Contract Accepted (Day 1)

    Now you can lock. Most lenders require an address — until you're under contract, you're "shopping," not "buying."

  • 3

    Anytime in the Lock Window

    You can lock on Day 1, Day 14, or wait until Day 25. The decision is yours — within your lender's policy.

  • 4

    Final Lock Required (~7 Days Out)

    Most lenders require a final lock 7–10 days before closing — they need the rate to draw final documents.

Lock & shop: Some lenders offer a "lock & shop" program where you can lock before you're under contract. Useful in a rising-rate environment, but typically costs an extra 0.125–0.25% on the rate.
0.25%
The Stakes

What a quarter-point actually costs.

On a $400,000 loan over 30 years, a 0.25% higher rate adds roughly $20,000 in interest — and about $60/month to your payment.

That's the rough scale of what you're trading when you lock vs. float. Worth thinking carefully about. Not worth losing sleep over.

2What Moves Rates

Mortgage rates follow bonds, not the Fed.

This is the single most important thing to understand — and the thing the news gets wrong every week. Your rate tracks the bond market, specifically Mortgage-Backed Securities (MBS).

When investors buy more MBS (because they're worried about the economy and want safe assets), prices go up and yields — your mortgage rate — go down.

When investors sell MBS (because the economy is hot and they want stocks instead), prices fall and yields — your mortgage rate — go up.

Good news for the economy is bad news for mortgage rates. Strong jobs report, hot inflation, booming GDP — every one of them pushes rates higher.

The Bond Market, every Friday
The five events that move rates the most
CPI

Consumer Price Index

Monthly inflation print. The single biggest market mover. Hot CPI → rates spike. Cool CPI → rates drop.

JOBS

Non-Farm Payrolls

First Friday of every month. Strong jobs report = strong economy = rates up. Weak report = rates down.

FED

FOMC Meetings

Eight times a year. The Fed Funds Rate matters less than the language — markets price in the path, not the cut itself.

GDP

GDP Releases

Quarterly. Confirms whether the economy is actually slowing or running hot — moves rates either way.

CRISIS

Geopolitical Risk

War, banking stress, anything that drives a "flight to safety" pulls money into bonds — rates drop fast.

4The Real Cost

What you're actually trading.

Two worked examples — same buyer, same property, same lender. One locks early. One floats and gets lucky. One floats and gets unlucky.

Scenario A · Lock Early

Day 1 lock — certainty wins

Buyer is under contract on a $450K home. Locks at 6.85% on Day 1 of contract. Closes Day 30 at the same rate. The market moved — but it didn't matter to her.

Loan amount$425,000
Locked rate (Day 1)6.85%
Rate at closing (Day 30 market)7.15%
Savings from locking early$25,500 over 30 yr

She didn't pick the bottom — she just removed the question. Most buyers should think of locking this way.

Scenario B · Float and Win

Floated through CPI — caught the cooling print

Buyer floats from Day 1, watches the bond market, locks the morning after a cool CPI. Caught 0.30% lower than Day 1.

Day 1 market rate6.85%
Locked rate (Day 12, post-CPI)6.55%
Savings from floating$25,500 over 30 yr

Worked out — but the buyer was watching the market and listening to her loan officer. Floating on autopilot is gambling.

Scenario C · Float and Lose

Floated into a hot CPI — got caught

Same buyer, different CPI surprise. Print came in hot, MBS sold off, lock window closing on her. Locked at 7.20% on Day 21 just to get to closing.

Day 1 market rate6.85%
Locked rate (Day 21, forced)7.20%
Cost vs. locking Day 1$30,000 over 30 yr

Same buyer, two CPI prints, two completely different outcomes. The difference between B and C is not skill — it's data luck.

5Float-Down Options

The "have your cake" feature most buyers don't ask about.

A float-down lets you lock for protection — and re-lock at a lower rate if the market drops significantly. It's the closest thing to a free lunch in lending.

1×

One-time use

Most lenders give you exactly one float-down per loan. Use it wisely — usually waiting until at least a 0.25% drop.

Δ

Minimum delta

Typical trigger is a 0.25% rate drop from your locked rate. Smaller drops won't qualify.

$

The cost

Some lenders bake it in at no charge. Others charge 0.125% on the locked rate, or a flat fee ($500–$1,500).

⏱

Timing window

Usually must be exercised at least 7–10 days before close. Past that, the rate is final.

The smart-money play: Lock on contract acceptance. Then watch the next CPI or jobs report. If the data pushes rates down 0.25%+, exercise the float-down. You get protection and upside.
Watch out: Always ask "is there a float-down on this loan, and what's the cost and trigger?" before you lock. Some lenders don't offer one. Some only offer it at significant cost.
6Decision Framework

The four questions that decide it for you.

Skip the gut feel. Run through these four checks, in order, every time.

1. How many days to close?
Lock

Under 30 days

You don't have time for a meaningful float thesis to play out. Lock and move on.

Float-Candidate

30+ days

You have at least one data release ahead of you. Floating becomes a real choice.

2. What's the next data print?
Lock

Major event < 48 hours away

CPI, jobs, FOMC. Don't gamble going in — lock before or wait until after.

Float-Candidate

Calm calendar for 2+ weeks

No major events = lower daily volatility. Floating costs less in tail risk.

3. What's the rate trend?
Lock

10-day trend rising

Trending up = market thinks the economy is hot or inflation is sticky. Lock now.

Float-Candidate

10-day trend falling

Trending down = market is pricing in cooling. Floating with discipline can capture more.

4. How would a 0.5% bump affect you?
Lock

It kills the deal

If a higher rate breaks your DTI or pushes the payment above what you can stomach, always lock.

Float-Candidate

It's a $90/month annoyance

If you can absorb it without rethinking the purchase, you have the freedom to float.

7Lock Extensions

If closing slips, your lock is the cost.

Your lock has an expiration date. If your closing moves past it — appraisal delay, title issue, repair holdup — you need an extension. They aren't free.

Typical cost
  • $

    7-day extension

    Around 0.06%–0.10% in points (~$240–$400 on a $400K loan).

  • $

    15-day extension

    Around 0.125% in points (~$500 on a $400K loan).

  • $

    30-day extension

    Around 0.25% in points (~$1,000 on a $400K loan).

How to avoid them
  • ✓

    Pick the right lock length

    If your contract is 45 days, lock for 45 — not 30. Extensions cost more than buying the longer lock up front.

  • ✓

    Push the agent to file early

    Title, HOA docs, repair receipts — every missing item buys delay risk.

  • ✓

    Know your lender's policy

    Some lenders absorb the first extension. Some charge from day one. Ask before you sign.

The exception: If rates have dropped since you locked and you'd extend anyway, ask your LO about re-locking at the new lower rate. Sometimes a "re-lock" beats an extension.
8Common Mistakes

The five mistakes that cost buyers real money.

In a decade of watching this decision play out across thousands of loans, the same five mistakes show up over and over.

  • 1

    Trying to pick the bottom

    You won't. Even the bond market doesn't. The right question isn't "can I get a lower rate?" — it's "can I live with this rate?"

  • 2

    Floating into a major data release

    If your lock has to happen this week and CPI prints tomorrow, that's not floating — that's gambling.

  • 3

    Not asking about float-down

    The smartest hedge in lending. Half of buyers don't know it exists. Almost none ask about it before locking.

  • 4

    Locking for the wrong window

    30-day lock on a 45-day contract is asking for an extension. Match the lock to the contract, not to the cheapest option.

  • 5

    Treating the Fed Funds Rate as the mortgage rate

    "They cut today, my rate should be lower tomorrow." Wrong. Mortgage rates often move opposite the Fed announcement.

Reference

Lock-and-rate language, decoded.

Every term you'll hear from your loan officer, in plain English.

LockRate Lock
A binding agreement that secures your interest rate for a set window (typically 30–60 days), independent of market moves.
FloatFloating Rate
Choosing not to lock — leaving your rate exposed to daily market changes until you lock or the lender requires it.
MBSMortgage-Backed Security
The bond that actually determines your mortgage rate. Pools of home loans sold to investors. When MBS prices rise, rates fall.
CPIConsumer Price Index
Monthly inflation report. The single biggest mortgage-rate mover. Released around the 10th–14th of each month.
FOMCFederal Open Market Committee
The Fed's rate-setting body. Meets 8× a year. Its statement often matters more than the decision itself.
Float-DownFloat-Down Option
A feature that lets you re-lock at a lower rate after the market drops by a minimum trigger (usually 0.25%).
Lock PeriodLock Window
The number of days your rate is guaranteed. Longer locks cost more (in points or a higher base rate).
ExtensionLock Extension
If closing slips past lock expiration, the buyer pays to extend — typically ~0.125% in points per 15 days.
10-Year10-Year Treasury
Government bond that broadly correlates with 30-year mortgage rates. A useful daily proxy — but MBS is the precise driver.
BPSBasis Point
One-hundredth of a percent. "Rates moved 25 bps" = a 0.25% change. The unit loan officers actually speak in.
PointsDiscount Points
Up-front cost paid to lower the rate. 1 point = 1% of loan amount, typically buys ~0.25% off the rate.
Re-LockRe-Lock
Breaking your current lock to start a new one at the prevailing rate. Allowed by some lenders; can be cheaper than an extension.
